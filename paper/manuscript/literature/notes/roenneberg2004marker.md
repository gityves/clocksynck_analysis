# roenneberg2004marker
**Ref:** Roenneberg, Kuehnle, Pramstaller, Ricken, Havel, Guth, Merrow, Curr Biol 2004 (14:R1038). DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2004.11.039. Type: clinical (human chronotype, questionnaire; Correspondence). Lab: Roenneberg (Munich).
**Source:** full text (short correspondence; figure values are not in the text)

## Question and design
How does chronotype change with age and sex? Munich ChronoType Questionnaire (MCTQ) database, N of about 25,000, mainly Germany and Switzerland, open (non-representative) age distribution; plus about 800 people in three secluded South Tyrol valleys. Chronotype = midpoint of sleep on free days corrected for sleep debt (MSFsc). **Sampling:** cross-sectional self-report, no physiological timing.

## Method (if any)
Questionnaire phenotype (MSFsc) averaged per age group and sex; t-tests against the age-20 group. Self-assessed childhood and teenage chronotype is used to argue a cohort effect is unlikely.

## Key results
- Direction: chronotype delays through childhood and adolescence to a maximum around age 20, then advances for the rest of life: "After 20, they become earlier again (advancing) with increasing age."
- Maximum of lateness: women 19.5 y, men 20.9 y (text); the sex difference (women earlier) disappears around age 50, near the average age of menopause.
- Almost all age groups differ from age 20 (t-test, p < 0.001, except ages 19, 21, 22 and 23).
- Elderly (> 65 y): "the data points show a large scatter", attributed to few subjects, less regular social and light schedules or a less robust circadian system.
- Age-dependent hormonal timing: growth hormone maximum (and cortisol minimum) near 1 a.m. in 16-25 y, "approximately one hour later than in the elderly (> 70 y)".
- Cohort vs age: self-recalled chronotypes mimic the age curve, so the pattern is taken to be similar over the last 70 years; the South Tyrol sample (N about 800) gives practically the same curve (data not shown).

## Not addressed / limits
- No size in hours of the advance per decade is given in the text (only the figure, not extracted).
- the study, being cross-sectional, cannot distinguish age from cohort; "Our data also cannot formally rule out behavioral and environmental factors" behind the age and sex differences.
- Sleep timing is behavioural; chronotype is a proxy for entrained phase, not a measure of internal clock time. Nothing on tissue clocks.

## Link to us
- **Intro claim(s):** after about 20 y, entrained sleep timing becomes earlier with age.
- **Relation to clocksync:** background / supports for A234. The direction (advance with age after 20) matches our finding that internal time runs ahead of clock time (+0.64 h per decade body, +0.99 h brain in GTEx). Behavioural light timing (earlier sleep, earlier light exposure) is a candidate cause alternative to lower central amplitude; the paper itself says it cannot exclude lifestyle.

## Ideas
- Pair with Czeisler 1999 (same tau at both ages, yet earlier phase): the advance is not a period effect.
- Get the slope of MSFsc per decade (in hours) from the figure or the supplemental data to compare with our 0.64 h per decade.
- GTEx has no chronotype. Check whether a purely behavioural account predicts the body versus brain difference (0.64 vs 0.99 h per decade) or only an amplitude/entrainment account does.
